NotUber

complete

You have joined the Data-Driven Algorithms Design Group at NotUber, a ride-hailing company in New York City. Passengers request rides from an app. Drivers sign in whenever they feel like driving. Matching has been outsourced to somebody else, and your job is to replace them.

Underneath the framing it is live bipartite matching over the NYC road network: an unpredictable stream of passengers appearing anywhere, an unpredictable pool of drivers going on and off shift, and a decision to make every time the two meet. There is no correct answer, only a trade-off between two things you cannot maximise at once — how long passengers wait, and how much drivers earn.

We built five strategies against the same simulation and measured both. Match the first free driver to the first waiting passenger. Then match on straight-line distance. Then stop pretending the city is open ground and match on actual drive time through the road graph with Dijkstra's. Then make it fast: preprocess every node into a KD-tree so snapping a person to their nearest road node is a spatial lookup rather than a linear scan, and swap Dijkstra for A* so the search is directed instead of expanding uniformly. Finally, stop treating availability as binary — also consider drivers within ten minutes of finishing a fare, and wait for one if they will arrive sooner than anybody idle.

The results are less flattering to sophistication than you would hope. Brute force averaged over thirteen hours of passenger wait and served 2,470 people. Simply matching on proximity — the crudest geometric idea available — collapsed mean wait to about twenty minutes and nearly doubled the number of rides. That one step is essentially the entire win.

Everything after it lands between 0.33 and 0.38 hours of mean wait, which is close enough that ranking them on wait time is not meaningful. KD-tree plus A* is actually fractionally worse on wait than plain Dijkstra's, and that is not a bug: they solve the same matching problem, so they produce similar assignments. The KD-tree and A* make finding the answer cheaper, not better. Where the last two strategies win is driver profit, which is what the fifth one was built for.

The honest gap: we never instrumented wall-clock time. We argued the complexity improvement and reported wait and profit, but never measured the speedup that was the entire point of strategy four. That is the first thing I would fix.

The interesting decision is the fifth strategy, because it is the only one that admits the goals conflict. Deliberately making a passenger wait for a driver who is not free yet is a worse decision for that passenger and a better one for the system. That is the step from implementing an algorithm to deciding what you are optimising for.

Python in Jupyter — numpy, pandas, scipy for the KD-tree, heapq for the priority queues. The notebooks are public, one per strategy.
