The premise handed to us: you have joined the algorithms group at a ride-hailing company in New York. Passengers appear on a map and request rides. Drivers sign in whenever they feel like driving. Matching has been outsourced, and your job is to replace the system that does it.

Underneath the framing it's a live matching problem over the real New York road network, with two goals that can't both be maximised — how long passengers wait, and how much drivers earn. There is no correct answer, only positions on a trade-off. So we built five strategies against the same simulation and measured both.

The first is deliberately stupid: match the first waiting passenger to the first free driver, ignoring geography entirely. The second computes straight-line distance to every available driver and takes the nearest. The third stops approximating and matches on actual shortest drive *time* through the road graph, using Dijkstra's. The fourth keeps that idea but makes it cheap — every intersection preprocessed into a KD-tree so snapping a passenger to their nearest road node is a spatial lookup rather than a scan of the whole city, and A\* in place of Dijkstra's so the search is pulled toward the destination instead of expanding blindly in all directions. The fifth stops treating drivers as simply free or busy: a driver a few minutes from finishing a fare might still reach the passenger first, so it weighs waiting for them against dispatching someone idle.

**The result that stuck with me is how early the gains arrive.** Brute force left passengers waiting over thirteen hours on average and cleared barely half as many rides. Adding the crudest possible notion of distance — a straight line, ignoring that New York has roads — collapsed that to about twenty minutes. Everything after it, including the genuinely clever version, moved the wait time by a rounding error.

That isn't an argument against the KD-tree and A\*. They're runtime optimisations; they make finding the answer cheaper, not the answer better, and the fifth strategy did meaningfully lift driver earnings. But it's a useful thing to have learned early: **most of the benefit usually comes from the first idea that isn't dumb, and sophistication after that has to justify itself against a baseline that's already good.**

The honest gap is that we never instrumented wall-clock time, so we argued the complexity improvement without ever measuring the speedup that was the whole point of the fourth strategy. That's the first thing I'd fix.
