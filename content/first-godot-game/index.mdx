The first thing I made in a game engine: dodge the mobs, survive, watch the score climb.

A player you steer around the screen, enemies that spawn off the edges and drift across, a collision that ends the run, and a HUD keeping score and putting the start message up between attempts. That's the whole game, and being the whole game is the point — it's small enough that every piece is visible at once.

What it taught was the engine's model rather than anything about games. Godot organises everything as scenes made of nodes, where a scene can be a whole level or a single reusable object, and instancing one inside another is how you compose a game. The player is a scene. A mob is a scene. The main level is a scene that spawns the others. Once that clicked — that there's no distinction between "a level" and "an object", only nodes and the trees you assemble them into — the rest of the engine stopped feeling arbitrary.

Signals were the other idea worth carrying away: nodes announce that something happened and don't care who's listening. The player says it was hit; the main scene decides that means the game is over. That's a habit that transfers well beyond a game engine, and it's most of why the platformer that came after this one was straightforward to put together.
